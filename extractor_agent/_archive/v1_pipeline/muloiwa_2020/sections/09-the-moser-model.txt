The Moser model is an unstructured model based on substrate concentration only introduced in 1958 by Herman Moser ( Moser, 1958) . The model is an extension of the Monod model. The Moser model introduced a parameter constant (n) on the Monod model. The parameter (n) is an adjustable parameter that provides flexibility in fitting experimental data and dynamic behaviour in the bioreactor ( Mahanta et al., 2014) . The Moser model is considered to be a better model than the Monod model because the parameter (n) can be adjusted to fit the experimental data ( Kong, 2017) . Just like the Blackman model, and Contois model, the Moser model has not been widely used to describe specific growth rate. The Moser model does not consider substrate inhibition, hence it cannot deal with toxic substrate. On the contrary, Krishnan et al., (2017) reported that the parameter (n) of the Moser model is used to describe substrate inhibition. Unlike the Monod model, and Contois model, the Moser model is capable of describing the lag phase when substrate concentration is high ( Kong, 2017) . The Moser model cannot describe the death phase.

Literature has shown good performance of the Moser model when fitted on different types of experimental data. Annuar et al., (2008) reported satisfactory fit on biosynthesis of medium-chain-length poly- (3-hydroxyalkanoates) in Pseudomonas putida. The 600 nm was used to measure the bacteria growth rates. The Moser model produced R² 95.7%. Choi and Lee (2018) reported a satisfactory fit on micro-algal biomass/bioenergy production with agricultural by-products. The cell dry weight method was used to measure the bacteria growth rates. The Moser model produced R² 96%. Dutta et al., (2015) reported a satisfactory fit on Cutinase producing Pseudomonas cepacia using tomatopeel extracted cutin. The OD at 600 nm was used to measure the bacteria growth rates. The Moser model produced R² 85.5%. Gharibzahedi et al., (2013) reported a satisfactory fit on cell growth and Canthaxanthin biosynthesis by Dietzia natronolimnaea HS-1. The OD at 600 nm was used to measure the bacteria growth rates. The Moser model produced R² 94.9%. Halmi et al., (2014) reported a satisfactory fit on catechol-degradation. The OD at 600 nm was used to measure the bacteria growth rates. The Moser model produced R² 99.2%. Krishnan et al., (2017) reported satisfactory fit on biodegradation of Azo dye mixture. The OD at 600 nm was used to measure the bacteria growth rates. The Moser model produced R² 96.1%. Sadhukhan et al., (2016) reported a satisfactory fit on microbial production of succinic acid using crude and purified glycerol from a crotalaria juncea. The OD at 600 nm was used to measure the bacteria growth rates. The Moser model produced RMSE 0.227. Mahanta et al., (2014) reported a satisfactory fit on E. coli. The Moser model produced R² of 99.96%. Ardestani (2011) reported a satisfactory fit on cell growth Pseudomonas putida ATCC 16,024. The cell dry weight method was used to measure the bacteria growth rates. The Moser model produced R² of 87%. Ardestani and Sha fiei (2014) reported a satisfactory fit on Saccharomyces cerevisiae cell growth. The cell dry weight method was used to measure the bacteria growth rates. The Moser model produced R² of 97%. Ardestani and Kasebkar (2018) reported a satisfactory fit on

Aspergillus niger PTCC 5010 in a batch culture. The cell dry weight method was used to measure the bacteria growth rates. The Moser model produced R² of 91.3%. On the other hand, Debasmita and Rajasimman (2013) reported a poor fit on biodegradation of Atrazine using mixed microorganisms. The growth rates were measured using OD at 600 nm to describe the specific growth rates. The Moser model produced R² 0.39%. The Moser model performed well on substrate that exhibits cell inhibition such as Azo dye, agricultural products, and succinic acid. The parameter n assisted when the model was subjected to toxic substrate.
